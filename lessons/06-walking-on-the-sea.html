Walking on the Sea

Because Jesus is with us, we can take bold steps of faith.

Context

Immediately after feeding the crowd, Jesus makes the disciples get in the boat and go ahead of Him, then finally gets the solitude He sought — up the mountain, alone, to pray. The Gospels show this as a habit, not an exception.

These squalls on the Sea of Galilee were familiar to them, and several of these men had already watched Jesus silence one (Mark 4:35-41). They were terrified anyway.

The passage points three separate times to Jesus's divinity in a handful of verses: He does what Job 9:8 says God alone does — treads on the waves; He says "It is I," which in Greek is the same phrase behind the divine name in Exodus 3:14; and the men in the boat call Him the Son of God.

Commentary

Matthew 14:22-23

"He made the disciples get into the boat." The wording is forceful. He sent them into the night crossing on purpose, and He was praying while they fought the wind.

Matthew 14:25

The fourth watch is roughly 3 to 6 a.m. They had been rowing against the wind most of the night before He came. The delay is part of the account, not an oversight.

Matthew 14:27

"It is I" translates egō eimi — "I AM." Read against Exodus 3:14, the reassurance is a claim about who is walking toward them.

Matthew 14:29-31

Peter walks. That is the part usually skipped in the retelling. He sinks when his attention shifts from Jesus to the wind, and Jesus catches him immediately — before the rebuke, not after it.

Matthew 14:33

"Truly you are the Son of God." In Mark's parallel the disciples are simply astounded; Matthew records worship. It is the first time in this Gospel that the disciples confess it together.

Questions

Why did Jesus send the disciples ahead into a night crossing?
Not carelessly. He had already calmed one storm in front of these men. He intended to reveal more of Himself in this one. Being sent somewhere hard is not evidence of being forgotten.
He moved toward them before they saw Him. Why does that detail matter?
They felt abandoned while He was already closing the distance. He moved first here, and He moved first in salvation — Romans 5:6-8 puts the cross before our asking.
How does fear change what we see?
It edits reality. They saw a rescuer and concluded ghost. Anxiety makes the worst-case interpretation feel like the obvious one, and it drowns out what we know is true.
Why did Jesus call Peter out before calming the storm?
It was a test in the sense of revealing, not grading. The invitation came while the wind was still up. God's call rarely waits for conditions to improve.
Peter sank. Is that a failure story?
Partly — he stopped continually trusting and started calculating. But he is the one who got out of the boat, and Jesus's hand was there instantly. The remedy for wavering faith isn't more effort; it's fixing your eyes on Jesus (Hebrews 12:2).
What does bold faith look like for you right now?
Usually something specific and uncomfortable: a confession, an apology, a conversation about Jesus with a friend, rearranging a schedule, walking away from something that has too much of you. Whatever the step is, He goes with you.

Deeper study

Themes, hard questions, word studies, cross-references

Summary

After the feeding, Jesus sends the disciples across the lake and goes up a mountain to pray. Before dawn He comes to them walking on the water. They think He is a ghost; He identifies Himself. Peter asks to come, walks, sinks in fear, and is caught. When Jesus enters the boat the wind stops, and the disciples worship Him as the Son of God.

Themes in the bigger story

  • Divine identity disclosed through action — walking on the sea is something the Old Testament reserves to God.
  • Presence rather than exemption: Jesus does not spare them the crossing; He comes to them in it.
  • The prayer life of Jesus, repeatedly noted in the Gospels and rarely imitated.
  • Worship as the fitting response to recognizing who He is, which becomes the pattern of the church.

Hard or debated

Is "O you of little faith" a rebuke or an endearment?

Jesus rescues first and speaks second. The phrase appears several times in Matthew for disciples who have real but wobbly faith. It names a deficiency without disowning the person — Peter is still held by the hand while he hears it.

Should we "get out of the boat"?

This story gets used as a slogan for risk-taking generally. Note that Peter went only because Jesus said "Come." The passage commends responding to Christ's call, not manufacturing risk to prove faith.

Why does Jesus pray if He is God?

A genuine puzzle. The historic answer is that the incarnate Son, fully human, lived in real dependence on the Father — prayer as authentic relationship within the Trinity, not performance.

Key words

ἐγώ εἰμιegō eimiGreek

"It is I." Ordinary Greek for self-identification, and also the phrase the Greek Old Testament uses to render God's self-naming in Exodus 3:14. John's Gospel builds a whole series of sayings on it.

φάντασμαphantasmaGreek

"A ghost" — an apparition. Their first instinct was the supernatural, just the wrong supernatural.

ὀλιγόπιστεoligopisteGreek

"You of little faith" — literally small-faithed. Little, not absent; Matthew uses it only of disciples.

ἐδίστασαςedistasasGreek

"Doubt" — from a root meaning "two," the idea of being pulled in two directions. Not unbelief but divided attention, which is exactly what happened when Peter looked at the wind.

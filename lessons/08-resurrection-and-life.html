Resurrection and Life

Because Jesus has authority over life and death, we can trust Him entirely.

Death Has Lost

38Jesus therefore, again groaning in himself, came to the tomb. Jesus said to them, “Free him, and let him go.”

Context

A common belief in the ancient Near East held that the soul lingered near the body for three days. By day four, hope was formally over. John notes the four days twice — Lazarus is not borderline, he is unambiguously dead.

Bethany was about two miles from Jerusalem, close enough that a crowd of mourners from the city had come out. Whatever happens here happens in front of witnesses.

This is the last and greatest of the signs in John's Gospel before the cross, and it is the one that sets the plot against Jesus in motion. Raising a man from the dead is what finally makes killing Him urgent.

Commentary

John 11:21-22

Martha's "if you had been here" is grief and faith at once, and then: "but even now." She hasn't stopped believing; she just can't see the shape of it.

John 11:25-26

"I am the resurrection and the life." Martha believes in a resurrection on the last day — correct theology. Jesus moves the category from an event on a calendar to a person standing in front of her.

John 11:33-35

"Deeply moved" translates a word with anger in it. He is not merely sad; something in Him is indignant at death. And then the shortest verse in the Bible — the Lord of life weeping at a graveside He is about to empty.

John 11:39-40

Martha objects about the odor. She had confessed faith seventeen verses earlier and now stands at the tomb doing the math. Belief and reality collide, and Jesus doesn't disqualify her for it.

John 11:43-44

He does not ask or hope. He commands, and Lazarus obeys — bound hand and foot, walking. The last instruction is given to the living: "Unbind him, and let him go."

Questions

How did Mary and Martha react to the delay?
Martha said "even now" — grief with a door still open. Mary said the same first sentence and stopped there. Some of us reach for God's sovereignty when things don't make sense; some of us are flattened by sorrow and doubt. He didn't scold either of them.
Why did Jesus weep if He knew what He was about to do?
Because He is fully human and death genuinely grieved Him, because sin's damage to His creation makes Him indignant, and because the people He loved still didn't understand who He was. Knowing the ending doesn't cancel the grief of the middle.
Read v. 37. When have you thought God could have prevented suffering and didn't?
Everyone eventually does. Some things worth holding: God made a good world, and the brokenness traces to human rebellion (Genesis 3); we're all under sin's verdict (Romans 3:10, 6:23); and God often reveals Himself in suffering rather than around it (Job 42:5). He doesn't waste it — and He stepped into it Himself.
What does Martha's comment about the odor reveal?
That faith and experience can pull in opposite directions in the same person. If she struggled with Jesus standing right there, we should expect our own logic to argue with trust.
What does calling Lazarus out prove about His authority?
He commanded, and death complied. Only God has that authority over life. If death takes orders from Jesus, then death has lost its sting.
How should that change how we live now?
Believers grieve — Jesus did — but not without hope. Death becomes a doorway rather than an ending. That frees you to live fully committed rather than defensively, and to handle disappointment knowing it isn't final.

Deeper study

Themes, hard questions, word studies, cross-references

Summary

Jesus arrives at Bethany four days after Lazarus's death. He meets Martha with the claim "I am the resurrection and the life," weeps with Mary at the tomb, and then calls Lazarus out of the grave in front of a crowd of mourners.

Themes in the bigger story

  • The seventh and climactic sign in John, pointing to Jesus's own resurrection and triggering the plot to kill Him.
  • Resurrection relocated from a doctrine about the future to a person in the present.
  • Grief taken seriously — the shortest verse in the Bible sits inside the greatest miracle.
  • Glory revealed through what looks like delay and failure, a pattern that culminates at the cross.

Hard or debated

Why did Jesus deliberately wait?

John 11:6 says He stayed two days longer after hearing Lazarus was ill. He states the reason in v. 15 — so that they may believe. That is honest and still hard: the sisters' days of grief were not an accident. Scripture presents purpose in the delay without pretending the delay was painless.

What did Lazarus experience?

The text says nothing, and neither does Lazarus anywhere in Scripture. Any account of what he saw is speculation. Note also that he died again — this is resuscitation to the same life, not resurrection to the new one.

Why does raising a man lead to plotting murder?

John 11:45-53 records the council's reasoning: the movement would grow, Rome would respond, and the nation would suffer. The greatest act of life in the Gospel directly produces the decision to kill Jesus.

Key words

ἐνεβριμήσατοenebrimēsatoGreek

"Deeply moved in his spirit" (v. 33). The word carries anger or snorting indignation — used elsewhere of a stern warning. Jesus is not only sorrowful at the tomb; He is angry at death.

ἐδάκρυσενedakrysenGreek

"Jesus wept" — quiet tears, a different word from the loud wailing (klaiō) used of the mourners around Him. His grief is real and not performative.

ἀνάστασιςanastasisGreek

"Resurrection" — literally a standing up again. Jesus does not say He brings it or announces it; He says He is it.

ζωήzōēGreek

"The life." John's preferred word for the life God gives, running from 1:4 through 20:31 — not merely biological existence.

Elsewhere in Scripture

Ezekiel 37:12-14
God opening graves and raising His people — the prophetic picture behind this scene.
Isaiah 26:19
"Your dead shall live" — the hope Martha already held.
John 5:28-29
Jesus's own earlier claim that the dead will hear His voice and come out.
1 Corinthians 15:20-26
Christ as firstfruits; death as the last enemy destroyed.
Romans 8:22-23
Creation groaning, awaiting the redemption of our bodies.
Revelation 21:4
Death itself abolished — where this sign is pointing.

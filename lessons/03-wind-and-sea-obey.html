Storms Reveal God's Authority
Mark 4:39-41read in ESV →

39He awoke, and rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, “Peace! Be still!” The wind ceased, and there was a great calm. 40He said to them, “Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?” 41They were greatly afraid, and said to one another, “Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?”

World English Bible — public domain.

Context

Mark is building a case. Chapter by chapter he stacks up demonstrations of Jesus's authority — over sickness, over demons, over nature, and finally over death — so that the reader arrives at the disciples' question with real force behind it.

The Sea of Galilee sits about 700 feet below sea level, ringed by hills. Cold air off the heights collides with warm air over the water and produces violent squalls with almost no warning. Several men in that boat fished this lake for a living, and they were certain they were about to die.

Early tradition holds that Mark drew his material from Peter, who was in the boat. The rawness of the disciples' accusation — "don't you care?" — reads like someone's actual memory rather than a tidied-up account.

Commentary

Mark 4:35

Crossing at evening was Jesus's idea. Whatever the storm was, it was not an interruption of the plan.

Mark 4:38

Asleep on the cushion in the stern. Mark records Jesus's exhaustion without embarrassment — He is fully human, and the humanity is not a costume.

Mark 4:39

"He rebuked the wind" uses the same verb Mark uses for casting out demons, and "Peace! Be still!" is literally "be muzzled." Jesus addresses the storm the way He addresses an unclean spirit. In the Old Testament, only God rules the raging sea.

Mark 4:41

They were more afraid after the calm than during the storm. Terror of the wind is ordinary; terror of the One who silences it is the beginning of understanding who is in the boat.

Questions

What were the disciples actually afraid of — and what did they forget?
They feared drowning, but the accusation underneath is "you don't care." They knew Jesus; they had not grasped His identity or His authority. Fear told them His sleep meant indifference.
Was the storm a surprise to Jesus? Then why sleep through it?
No. He proposed the crossing. He slept because He was genuinely tired — fully man — and because He was in no danger — fully God. Both are true at once, and that combination is the comfort: He understands it and He commands it.
Why does the manner of the calming matter?
A word. No ritual, no appeal to God for help. In the Psalms it is the LORD who stills the sea, so Mark's readers are meant to draw the obvious conclusion — and the disciples nearly do.
When have you struggled to sense God's presence in a storm?
Worth saying out loud. The hospital room, the divorce, the loss, the stretch of loneliness. He isn't unaware of it. Romans 8:28 doesn't promise the storm is good; it promises He is working in it.
Why is trusting God hard mid-storm, and can storms help rather than hurt our view of Him?
Because you can't see the way out. Jesus can calm every storm and often doesn't — He reveals more of Himself inside the wind than He would have in the calm. Storms tend to correct our sense of how much we need Him.
Why were they terrified after He calmed it?
Because the category they had for Jesus just broke. That moment is part of why these men later risked their lives carrying the gospel — they had seen it themselves.

Deeper study

Themes, hard questions, word studies, cross-references

Summary

Crossing the Sea of Galilee at Jesus's initiative, the disciples are caught in a squall violent enough to swamp the boat while Jesus sleeps. They wake Him with an accusation; He silences the storm with a word and asks about their faith. Their response is not relief but awe: who is this?

Themes in the bigger story

  • God's mastery of the chaotic sea, a thread running from Genesis 1 through the Psalms to Revelation 21:1, where there is no more sea.
  • The identity question that drives Mark's Gospel — answered by a centurion at the cross in 15:39.
  • Fear and faith as alternatives, not opposites of comfort. Jesus doesn't rebuke them for being in a storm.
  • The incarnation held together: the sleeping man and the commanding Lord are the same person in the same scene.

Hard or debated

Why doesn't Jesus calm every storm?

This passage proves He can, which makes the times He doesn't harder, not easier. Scripture doesn't give a formula. It gives Paul's thorn (2 Corinthians 12:7-10), Job's whirlwind, and the promise of presence rather than exemption.

Was Jesus's rebuke of the disciples harsh?

"Have you still no faith?" stings. Read in Mark's flow, it's diagnostic rather than punitive — He is naming the gap between what they had already seen and how they responded, and He calms the storm before He asks.

Did Jesus have limited knowledge?

If He is fully God, did He know the storm was coming? Mark 13:32 says the Son does not know the day or hour, which has occupied theologians for centuries. The historic answer runs through the incarnation — genuine human limitation taken on without loss of deity.

Key words

ἐπετίμησενepetimēsenGreek

"He rebuked." Mark uses this same verb for Jesus silencing demons (1:25). Treating the storm as something to be rebuked is a deliberate echo.

πεφίμωσοpephimōsoGreek

"Be still" — literally "be muzzled," a perfect passive imperative. The force is closer to "be gagged and stay that way."

λαῖλαψlailapsGreek

A whirlwind or squall, not steady weather. The same word appears in the Greek Old Testament for the whirlwind of God's judgment.

δειλοίdeiloiGreek

"Why are you so afraid?" — deilos is cowardice, a fear that fails rather than fear as an emotion. It's the opposite of the reverent "great fear" they feel in v. 41 (phobos).

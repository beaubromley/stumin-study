Context

Every Gospel tells the crucifixion, and each one lingers on different details. Mark, the spine of today's reading, is the starkest: no long speeches from the cross, just a handful of words, a wall of mockery, three hours of darkness, and one loud cry. The Roman centurion's confession is the first time in Mark's story that a human being calls Jesus the Son of God and means it as a conclusion rather than a question.

Crucifixion was Rome's public warning to slaves and rebels: this is what happens to people who challenge the empire. It was designed to be slow, shameful and visible. The sign over Jesus's head, "The King of the Jews," was Pilate's irony, an intended insult that turned out to be true. Jewish readers also knew that anyone hung on a tree was under a curse (Deuteronomy 21:23), which is why Paul later says Christ became a curse for us.

Today's passage joins four accounts. Mark gives the frame and the cry of abandonment. Luke adds the criminal who believes. John adds "I am thirsty" and "It is finished." Read together, they show a man in agony who is also fully in control of what is happening.

Commentary

Mark 15:21

Simon of Cyrene is pulled out of the crowd and made to carry the cross. He was a bystander on his way in from the country, and Mark names his sons as if the first readers knew them. The likely reading is that the family later became part of the church. A man who was forced into the story ended up inside it.

Mark 15:23

Wine mixed with myrrh was a mild painkiller, and Jesus refuses it. He chooses to face the full weight of what He came to do with a clear head.

Mark 15:24-28

Soldiers gamble for His clothes, the sign names Him king, and He hangs between two criminals. Mark's note, "He was counted with transgressors," quotes Isaiah 53:12. The King's throne is a cross and His court is two thieves.

Mark 15:29-32

The mockery comes from three directions: passersby, the religious leaders, and the men hanging next to Him. "He can't save himself" is the most ironic line in the Gospel. It is true, but not the way they mean it. He stays because He is saving others.

Luke 23:39-43

One of the two criminals joins the mockery and the other turns. He admits his guilt, says Jesus is innocent, and asks to be remembered. He has no good works to offer, no time to be baptized and nothing to bring but the request. Jesus's answer is immediate and personal: "today you will be with me in Paradise."

Mark 15:33-34

Darkness at midday. Then the cry, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?", which is the opening line of Psalm 22. It is not a loss of faith. It is a real cry from the place where sin's weight lands on Him, and it is still prayer, still addressed to "my God."

John 19:28-30

"It is finished." John's word is not a sigh of defeat. It is the announcement that the work is complete, the debt paid. Even His death is something He does: He "bowed his head, and gave up his spirit." Nobody takes His life from Him.

Mark 15:37-39

He cries out and dies, the temple curtain tears from top to bottom, and the centurion, a professional executioner, says, "Truly this man was the Son of God!" The tear runs from the top, so God did the tearing. The way into His presence is opened by the death itself.

Questions

Why do you think Jesus refused the wine mixed with myrrh?
He was not looking for a way around the suffering. He came to take it on fully and with full awareness. It is a small detail, but it shows that He went to the cross on purpose, awake to all of it.
Why is "He saved others; he can't save himself" such an ironic thing to say?
Because they had it backwards. He could have come down. He stayed because saving others required staying. The mockers thought weakness was proof He wasn't the Messiah, when the willingness to stay was the proof.
What does the second criminal teach us about how someone is saved?
Almost everything essential in a few sentences. He admitted that he was guilty, recognized that Jesus was not, and asked to be remembered. He had nothing to offer, and Jesus promised him paradise that same day. Salvation is a gift received by someone with empty hands, not a reward earned by someone with a good record.
What do you make of Jesus's cry, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"
It is a real cry, not a performance. He is quoting Psalm 22, a psalm that starts in anguish and ends in praise, and He is experiencing what it describes. The Son who always lived in unbroken fellowship with the Father takes the separation that our sin earned. It is one of the places in Scripture where we are meant to stop and admit we do not fully understand.
What does "It is finished" mean for us?
The work of saving us is complete. Nothing is left for us to add. We don't finish it with enough effort, and we can't undo it with enough failure. The right response is gratitude and trust, not an attempt to pay what He already paid.
The curtain tore and a soldier who had just helped kill Jesus confessed Him. What is Mark showing us?
That His death opens the way to God, and not just for the people we would expect. The curtain kept people out of God's presence, and now it is open. The first person to put it into words is an outsider who watched the whole thing. If He can reach that man, He can reach the people we think are too far gone.
Why does it matter that Jesus died, and did not just seem to?
Every account is careful about the details: He cried out, breathed His last, and a centurion who had seen many deaths confirmed it. The resurrection only means something if the death was real. It is why the next session, Go and Tell, begins with an empty tomb and not an empty cross.

Deeper study

Themes, hard questions, word studies, cross-references

Summary

Jesus is led out, crucified at Golgotha between two criminals, mocked by passersby, leaders and the men beside Him, and abandoned to darkness for three hours. One criminal repents and is promised paradise. Jesus cries out in the words of Psalm 22, says "It is finished," and gives up His spirit. The temple curtain tears in two, and a Roman centurion confesses that He was the Son of God.

Themes in the bigger story

  • The King who rules from a cross: power shown by what He is willing to endure.
  • Substitution: the innocent counted with transgressors, bearing what they earned.
  • Salvation as gift: a dying thief with nothing to offer and a promise he did not earn.
  • Access: the torn curtain and the confession of an outsider.
  • Death on His own terms: He is not overpowered, He completes what He came to do.

Hard or debated

The cry of abandonment

Christians have long disagreed about what was happening. Some say the Father truly turned away as Jesus bore sin. Others stress that He is quoting a psalm that ends in trust and that the Father never stopped loving the Son. Nearly all agree that Jesus took the full weight of our sin and that the cry was genuine. The Trinity was not broken, but something real and costly took place that we cannot fully explain.

Why does the sequence of hours differ between Mark and John?

Mark says Jesus was crucified at the third hour, and John places Pilate's judgment around the sixth hour. Common explanations are that John counts from midnight in Roman style while Mark counts from sunrise, or that the times are rounded and not meant to be exact. Nothing in the doctrine depends on resolving it.

Why did God require a death?

Scripture gives several overlapping pictures: sacrifice, ransom, substitution, victory over evil, and revealing God's love. Different traditions lean on different ones. The Gospels themselves do not choose one and every account shows a death that was both deliberate and for others.

"Today you will be with me in Paradise"

The punctuation of the Greek is not fixed, but the usual reading puts "today" with the promise. Jesus does not say when or how the criminal will be raised, only that fellowship with Him begins that day. The passage also shows that the thief's salvation required no ritual or works beyond trust.

Key words

τετέλεσταιtetelestaiGreek

"It is finished." The same verb was used for paying off a debt and for completing a task. The word looks back over the whole work and declares that nothing is left to do.

ἠλί ἠλί λιμὰ σαβαχθανίEloi, Eloi, lama sabachthaniAramaic

Jesus speaks Aramaic, the everyday language of Galilee, and the Gospel keeps His words. Mark preserves it, then translates it. Bystanders mistook "Eloi" for "Elijah," which is why they expected a rescue.

καταπέτασμαkatapetasmaGreek

"Veil" or "curtain," the heavy cloth in the temple that screened the Most Holy Place. Hebrews 10:19-20 reads its tearing as the opening of a new and living way into God's presence.

παράδεισοςparadeisosGreek

"Paradise," originally a Persian word for a walled garden. In the New Testament it names the place of the blessed presence of God, and it recalls the garden that was lost in Genesis.

Elsewhere in Scripture

Psalm 22
The psalm Jesus quotes from the cross. It describes pierced hands and feet, divided clothes and mocking crowds, and ends in praise.

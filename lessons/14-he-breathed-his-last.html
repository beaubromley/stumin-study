Context

Mark 15 is the climax of the Gospel's second half, and Mark tells it plainly. Pilate has just handed Jesus over to be crucified after the crowd chose Barabbas. What follows is a run of humiliations, six hours on a cross, and a burial, with very few words from Jesus. The point is not to spare the reader. Mark wants us to see exactly what Jesus endured.

The title everyone throws at Him is "King." The soldiers say it as a joke, the leaders say it as a taunt, and the sign over His head says it as a charge. All of them are mocking, and all of them are accidentally right. This is the whole irony of the chapter: the people ridiculing the King are acting out His coronation.

Crucifixion was Rome's public warning to slaves and rebels, built to be slow, shameful and visible. Mark's pace shows it: the hours are counted, the darkness falls over the whole land, and the section ends with a stone rolled across a tomb. Jesus's death is not a poetic gesture. It is a death with a body, a time of day and a burial, witnessed by people who could say where He was laid.

Commentary

Mark 15:16-17

The whole cohort, a few hundred soldiers, gathers for the entertainment. They dress Him in purple, the color of royalty, and weave a crown from thorns. Every piece of the costume is a parody of an emperor's court.

Mark 15:18-19

"Hail, King of the Jews!" echoes the "Hail, Caesar" cry used for the emperor. They strike Him, spit on Him and kneel in mock homage. Mark does not soften it, and the soldiers do not know that every gesture is aimed at the one who will actually be worshipped.

Mark 15:20

They take the purple off and put His own clothes back on, and lead Him out. The mockery is over and the execution begins. Jesus has said almost nothing through all of it.

Mark 15:29-30

The passersby shake their heads and say, "Save yourself, and come down from the cross!" They are repeating the accusation from His trial about the temple. They assume that a real Messiah would escape suffering. They have it backwards.

Mark 15:31-32

"He saved others. He can't save himself." It is the most ironic line in the Gospel: true, but not the way they mean it. He stays on the cross because He is saving others. The leaders say they will believe if He comes down, but staying is the sign they cannot see. Even the men crucified beside Him join in.

Mark 15:33-34

Darkness falls at noon and lasts three hours. Then the only words Mark records from the cross: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" These are the opening words of Psalm 22. It is a real cry from the place where sin's weight falls on Him, and it is still prayer, still addressed to "my God."

Mark 15:35-36

The bystanders mishear Him as calling Elijah, and someone offers sour wine on a sponge. Even at the end, He is misunderstood, and the one small act of kindness comes mixed with mockery.

Mark 15:37-38

He cries out and gives up His spirit, and the temple curtain tears from top to bottom. It tears from the top, so God did the tearing. The barrier between people and God's presence is removed by this death.

Mark 15:39

The centurion, a professional executioner, watches how Jesus died and says, "Truly this man was the Son of God!" It is the first time in Mark that a human being declares this as a conclusion. It comes from an outsider, at the foot of the cross.

Mark 15:40-41

The women who followed Jesus in Galilee are still there, watching from a distance. The disciples scattered, but these women stayed. They will be the witnesses to the burial and, in the next chapter, to the empty tomb.

Mark 15:42-47

Joseph of Arimathea, a member of the council, boldly asks Pilate for the body. Pilate confirms the death through the centurion. The burial is quick and careful: linen, a rock tomb, a stone. Mark is making sure we know that Jesus really died, so that what comes next can only be a resurrection.

Questions

Why do you think Mark spends so much time on the mockery before the crucifixion?
To show who Jesus is by what He is willing to endure. He is mocked as a king by people who have no idea they are right. The patience and the silence are the point: He could stop it, and He does not. The King receives the crown of thorns on purpose.
"He saved others. He can't save himself." Why is that such an ironic thing to say?
Because they had it backwards. He could have come down. He stayed because saving others required staying. The mockers thought weakness was proof He wasn't the Messiah, when the willingness to stay was the proof.
What do you make of Jesus's cry, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"
It is a real cry, not a performance. He is quoting Psalm 22, a psalm that starts in anguish and ends in praise, and He is experiencing what it describes. The Son who always lived in unbroken fellowship with the Father takes the separation that our sin earned. It is one of the places in Scripture where we are meant to stop and admit we do not fully understand.
What does the torn curtain tell us?
That the way to God is open. The curtain kept people out of the Most Holy Place, and it tears from the top, so God did it. Jesus's death is what opens access, and we don't have to earn our way in through anything but Him.
Why does it matter that a Roman soldier is the one who says, "Truly this man was the Son of God"?
Because it shows the cross reaches people we would write off. The one who helped carry out the execution is the first to get it right. If Jesus can reach that man, He can reach the people we think are too far from Him. It is also a challenge to those of us who have been around church our whole lives.
Why did Mark include the women and Joseph of Arimathea?
They are witnesses. The women watched Him die and saw where He was laid, and Joseph handled the body. It is the public record that Jesus was dead, which is what makes the resurrection a resurrection and not a rumor. They also show faithfulness in small things: staying close when everyone else ran.
What does this passage ask of us?
To take the cost seriously. Jesus endured torture, mockery and death on purpose, for us. We don't have to pretend it was easy or hurry past it. The right response is gratitude and trust, and a willingness to be as faithful in the hard moments as the women were.

Deeper study

Themes, hard questions, word studies, cross-references

Summary

After Pilate hands Him over, the soldiers mock Jesus as a king, beat Him and lead Him out to be crucified. On the cross He is ridiculed by passersby, the leaders and those hanging beside Him. Darkness covers the land for three hours, and Jesus cries out in the words of Psalm 22 and dies. The temple curtain tears in two, a centurion confesses that He was the Son of God, and Joseph of Arimathea buries the body while the women watch where He is laid.

Themes in the bigger story

  • The King who rules from a cross: power shown by what He is willing to endure.
  • Irony throughout: everything meant as mockery is accidentally true.
  • Substitution: the innocent taking on what others earned.
  • Access to God: the torn curtain and the confession of an outsider.
  • A real death: witnesses, a burial, and a sealed tomb.

Hard or debated

The cry of abandonment

Christians have long disagreed about what was happening. Some say the Father truly turned away as Jesus bore sin. Others stress that He is quoting a psalm that ends in trust and that the Father never stopped loving the Son. Nearly all agree that Jesus took the full weight of our sin and that the cry was genuine. The Trinity was not broken, but something real and costly took place that we cannot fully explain.

The hours of the day

Mark puts the darkness from the sixth to the ninth hour, which is noon to 3 p.m. John places Pilate's judgment around the sixth hour, which seems to conflict with Mark's "third hour" for the crucifixion in verse 25. Common explanations are that John counts from midnight in Roman style or that the times are rounded. Nothing in the doctrine depends on resolving it.

What the torn curtain means

Interpreters differ on which curtain Mark means, the one in front of the Most Holy Place or the outer one, and whether it signals judgment on the temple, access to God, or both. Hebrews reads it as opening a new way into God's presence. Mark's emphasis is that God tore it, from top to bottom.

Why did God require a death?

Scripture gives several overlapping pictures: sacrifice, ransom, substitution, victory over evil, and revealing God's love. Different traditions lean on different ones. Mark gives us the event and its effects without a theory, and every account shows a death that was both deliberate and for others.

Key words

ἠλί ἠλί λιμὰ σαβαχθανίEloi, Eloi, lama sabachthaniAramaic

Jesus speaks Aramaic, the everyday language of Galilee, and Mark keeps His words and then translates them. Bystanders mistook "Eloi" for "Elijah," which is why they expected a rescue.

καταπέτασμαkatapetasmaGreek

"Veil" or "curtain," the heavy cloth in the temple that screened the Most Holy Place. Hebrews 10:19-20 reads its tearing as the opening of a new and living way into God's presence.

ἐξέπνευσενexepneusenGreek

"Breathed his last," literally breathed out. Mark uses a plain word for a plain fact: Jesus actually died, which is what the centurion confirms to Pilate.

σπεῖραspeiraGreek

"Cohort," a Roman military unit of several hundred soldiers. Mark's point is that the whole garrison was there for the mockery.

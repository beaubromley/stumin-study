Our Faith Saves Us

Context

Luke braids two stories together. Jairus is a synagogue ruler — respectable, known, and taking a public risk by falling at Jesus's feet while religious leaders were lining up against Him. The woman is unnamed, broke after twelve years of doctors, and ceremonially unclean.

Her condition was not only medical. Under Levitical law, ongoing bleeding made her unclean, and anyone she touched became unclean too. Twelve years of that means twelve years outside worship and normal community life.

By the law's logic, her touch should have contaminated Jesus. It runs the other way. He is not made unclean; she is made whole. That reversal is the hinge of the passage.

Commentary

Luke 8:41-42

Jairus's daughter is twelve years old; the woman has bled for twelve years. Luke sets the number down twice on purpose — one life beginning, one life stalled, both restored in the same afternoon.

Luke 8:44

She reaches for the fringe of His garment — the tassels an observant Jewish man wore under Numbers 15:38-39. She approaches from behind, intending to take healing and disappear.

Luke 8:45-47

Jesus's question isn't for information. He stops a crowd, and a delay that will cost Jairus his daughter's life, so that she doesn't leave healed but still anonymous and ashamed.

Luke 8:48

"Daughter." The only time in the Gospels Jesus addresses a woman this way. Twelve years of exclusion end with a word of belonging, and "go in peace" is shalom — restored wholeness, not just an absence of symptoms.

Luke 8:50-55

"Do not fear; only believe," spoken to a man who has just been told it's over. The mourners laugh. Jesus calls death sleep — not a denial of the death but a claim about how temporary it is in His presence.

Questions

What does it mean to be desperate, and where do you go when you get there?
Desperation is hopelessness that drives you to do something drastic. Both of these people ran to Jesus. Most of us run somewhere with less power — a screen, a substance, a distraction. Those can numb; they cannot save.
Why did Jesus stop and ask who touched Him?
Not confusion. He wanted her, not just her healing. He refused to let her leave as an anonymous rule-breaker — He called her daughter in front of the crowd that had excluded her.
What does it mean that Jesus stopped for both a shunned woman and a synagogue ruler?
He crosses the whole social range in one scene. Status doesn't earn His attention and disgrace doesn't forfeit it. He was more concerned with drawing people in than with the purity rules that kept them out.
He said her faith saved her. What kind of faith is that?
Not believing Jesus existed — believing He was who He claimed and could do what He promised. Confessing Him as Lord (Romans 10:9-13), which starts a relationship that keeps changing you.
What kept Jairus believing after the news arrived?
He had just watched Jesus heal an incurable woman on the way. If that, then why not this? Notice the order of events — Luke arranges it so Jairus sees the greater case before he hears the worse news.
Do you struggle to be honest with God about your fears?
The woman tried to slip away unnoticed and trembled when she was found. Shame keeps us quiet. What she got was not a reprimand but a name — and He does not turn away people who come to Him.

Deeper study

Themes, hard questions, word studies, cross-references

Summary

On his way to heal a synagogue ruler's dying daughter, Jesus is interrupted by a woman who has bled for twelve years and touches His garment in the crowd. He stops, draws her out, calls her daughter, and sends her in peace — then arrives at Jairus's house after the girl has died and raises her.

Themes in the bigger story

  • Clean and unclean reversed: contact with Jesus purifies rather than contaminating, which anticipates the whole logic of the cross.
  • Faith as the thread linking two people who share nothing else — status, gender, or reputation.
  • Jesus's authority extending past sickness to death itself, preparing the reader for chapter 24.
  • Interruption as ministry, not obstacle. The delay that costs Jairus everything is where the woman is restored — and Jesus handles both.

Hard or debated

Did power leave Jesus automatically, without His consent?

"I perceive that power has gone out from me" can sound mechanical, as if the healing were magic tapped by touch. But He immediately makes it personal, and He credits her faith, not the fabric. The text presents an intentional act, not a leak.

"She is not dead but sleeping."

The mourners laugh because she was plainly dead. Jesus isn't correcting their diagnosis; He's stating what death amounts to when He is standing in the room. The New Testament goes on to use "sleep" for the death of believers (1 Thessalonians 4:13-14).

Why the command to tell no one?

Luke 8:56 has Jesus ordering silence right after a very public miracle. Mark's Gospel does this repeatedly. The usual explanation is timing — a reputation as a wonder-worker would have accelerated a confrontation, and distorted the kind of Messiah people expected.

Key words

σέσωκέν σεsesōken seGreek

"Your faith has made you well" — the verb is sōzō, "to save." The same word covers physical rescue and salvation from sin, and Luke is almost certainly using both senses. Hence the lesson's title.

κράσπεδονkraspedonGreek

The "fringe" or tassel of His cloak — the tzitzit commanded in Numbers 15:38-39 as a reminder of the commandments. She reached for the part of Him that signified covenant faithfulness.

θυγάτηρthygatērGreek

"Daughter." A term of belonging in a culture where she had been formally excluded. It is also the word used of Jairus's child, tying the two stories with one noun.

εἰρήνηeirēnēGreek

"Go in peace" renders the Hebrew shalom — not calm feelings but restored wholeness, back inside the community and its worship.
